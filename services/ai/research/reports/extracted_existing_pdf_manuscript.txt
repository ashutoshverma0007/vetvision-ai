Abstract—Lumpy skin disease (LSD) is a major disease that
poses a significant threat to the health and agricultural produc-
tivity of cattle, and rapid, easy screening is needed. Conventional
diagnosis requires veterinary skills and laboratory confirmation,
and image-based deep-learning systems can face problems with
computational efficiency, interpretability and generalization. This
paper presents VetVision AI, a deep-learning-based system to
classify cattle skin images as Normal Skin or Lumpy Skin.
It uses class-weighted learning, evaluation of pretrained con-
volutional neural networks, and efficient CPU inference with
ONNX Runtime. Grad-CAM is provided to aid visual analysis of
predictions. On a held-out test set of 153 images, EfficientNet-B0
achieved 90.85% accuracy, 89.25% macro-F1 and 91.98% ROC-
AUC. ONNX optimization reduced the median inference latency
from 55.78 ms per image to 19.92 ms per image, a 64.29%
decrease, while preserving prediction accuracy. The proposed
system is more useful in terms of deployment efficiency while
maintaining acceptable test accuracy, albeit slightly lower than
the other MobileNetV3 variant, MobileNetV3-Large. The findings
suggest the potential for CPU-based veterinary image screening,
but further studies, including larger datasets, independent farm-
level validation and prospective clinical assessment, are neces-
sary before implementation. VetVision AI is intended for initial
screening and should not be used to diagnose the condition.
Index Terms—Lumpy skin disease, cattle disease detection,
deep learning, EfficientNet-B0, MobileNetV3, ONNX Runtime,
explainable artificial intelligence, veterinary image classification,
CPU optimization.
I. INTRODUCTION
A. Background
Lumpy skin disease (LSD) is a viral condition of cattle that
results in skin nodules, loss of productivity and significant eco-
nomic losses. Its emergence in India has necessitated prompt
detection and appropriate management [1], [2]. Diagnosis is
usually made by clinical examination and laboratory tests, the
latter of which can be difficult to access in low-resource areas.
Recently, deep learning has been shown to enable automatic
recognition of LSD from image analysis [3]–[5]. Powerful
feature extractors such as EfficientNet and MobileNet [7]–
[9] and residual learning are available, spanning a range of
computational costs.
B. Motivation
Current methods yield promising classification outcomes
but are hampered by limited labeled datasets, computational
demands, interpretability and the transferability of classifi-
cation to different imaging situations [11], [14]. Alternative
representations, such as those offered by vision transformers,
can also be used but may be costly to compute, depending on
their configuration [10]. High classification accuracy does not
guarantee clinical reliability. Explainability techniques such
as Grad-CAM can highlight image regions relevant to the
prediction, aiding transparent assessment of models [12], [20].
These considerations motivate VetVision AI as an efficient,
interpretable and CPU-compatible screening system.
C. Problem Statement
Techniques currently employed to detect LSD are techni-
cally viable, but variations in datasets and evaluation protocols
produce differences in results that make direct comparison
difficult [3]–[5]. Small test sets, possible data leakage and in-
adequate external validation can produce inaccurate estimates
of generalization [14], [18]. A practical screening system
must therefore balance classification performance, inference
efficiency, interpretability and a clear distinction between
preliminary screening predictions and confirmed veterinary
diagnosis [15], [19].
D. Objective and Scope
This research aims to create VetVision AI to classify cattle
skin images as Normal Skin or Lumpy Skin. It evaluates
pretrained CNN architectures, explores class-weighted learn-
ing, studies accuracy, macro-F1 and AUC of classification,
and optimizes CPU inference using ONNX Runtime. Visual
interpretation is provided by Grad-CAM. Experimental report-
ing follows recommendations for transparent evaluation of AI
[16], [17]. The scope does not include disease-severity grading
or clinical diagnosis and is limited to image-based screening.
Real-world implementation of the vet vision involves indepen-
dent validation at animal level and farm level.

II. LITERATUREREVIEW
Lumpy skin disease virus (LSD virus) is an economically
important cattle disease that has severe implications for animal
health, as it results in the formation of skin nodules and
impact on productivity.Gupta et al. discussed the emergence
of LSD in India, and the importance of early diagnosis,
laboratory confirmation, and disease control [1]. Outbreak
investigations thereafter indicated that there are pathological
and epidemiological differences between the outbreaks, and
thus, visual screening should be employed in conjunction
with, and not instead of, veterinary examination and molecular
testing in India [2].
With the aid of deep learning, automated interpretation
of animal-health images is becoming increasingly common.
Senthilkumar et al. conducted a comparative study of the
pretrained convolutional neural networks (CNNs) and tested
several CNN architectures for LSD recognition, concluding
that MobileNetV2 [3] achieved good classification results to
be used for LSD recognition. In the field of detecting LSD,
Pal et al. investigated CNN and transformer approaches, which
have been a topic of increasing interest in the literature due
to their ability to extract features and strong representation
learning [4]. Ullah et al. suggested a vision-transformation
based mobile application that achieved 98.12% accuracy on
8,000 cattle images [5]. These findings, however, are not
directly comparable, due to differences in the composition of
the datasets, the ways in which they were split, the quality of
the disease labels, and external validation.
These systems are based on the methodological foundations
of hierarchical visual feature learning. AlexNet showed the
proof of the power of deep CNNs for image classification on
large scale images [6] and residual connections for optimizing
deeper network [7]. EfficientNet proposed a compound scaling
of depth, width and resolution that achieves a good tradeoff be-
tween accuracy and computation [8]. MobileNetV3 solved the
problem of resource-concstrained inference by designing the
architecture based on hardware [9]. Unlike Vision Transform-
ers, which use self-attention for long-range image relations,
they are often not more efficient in practice, depending on
the training data, implementation and computational resources
[10]. These developments help to compare lightweight CNNs
with alternatives based on transformers, not the most compli-
cated network is the best network for the field.
The benefits of transfer learning, where the number of
labeled images is limited, have been demonstrated in medical-
image-analysis studies, though the same studies revealed a
host of problems, including annotation quality, class imbalance
and distribution-shift [11]. With Grad-CAM, one can obtain
spatial explanations, which depict regions of the image that
the CNN considers relevant, but do not necessarily indicate
biological relevance [12]. Previous classification studies of
plant diseases have demonstrated that image-based analysis
can be effective, but also indicated that these techniques may
not be generalizable to real-world scenarios if the data is
carefully chosen [13].
One of the important points of generalization is particularly
true for veterinary applications. Zech et al. demonstrated that
medical-image classifiers can take advantage of the peculiar-
ities of the institution where they were trained and exhibit
inconsistent performance on test sets from other institutions
[14]. Kelly et al. identified workflow integration, reliability
and prospective evaluation as the challenges to scale AI
applications to real clinical environments [15]. The key re-
porting items that are highlighted in CLAIM and TRIPOD+AI
include well described datasets, the validation process, model
development and explicitly stated limitations [16] [17]. These
studies combined suggest that high accuracy does not ensure
clinical utility.
A. Research Gap
Although research already shows that image-based LSD
classification is technically feasible, gaps remain in compu-
tational efficiency, clinical interpretability, leakage-resistant
evaluation, and independent validation across animals, farms
and imaging conditions. A binary screening system should
also not be presented as a “severity-grading model” without
severity labels. To address these challenges, the proposed
VetVision AI research will combine an efficient pretrained
CNN, reproducible group-aware evaluation, class-sensitive
metrics, visual explanations and CPU-oriented deployment.
Before any clinical claims can be substantiated, independent
validation and broader field testing are required.
III. METHODOLOGY
A. Research Design
This research introduces VetVision AI, a deep-learning-
based binary classification model that distinguishes Lumpy
Skin (LS) from Normal Skin (NS) in photographs of cat-
tle skin. The approach combines dataset quality assessment,
transfer learning, comparative model evaluation and infer-
ence optimization. Transfer learning reduces the training time
needed by reusing pretrained visual representations [6], [7],
and lightweight architectures ease deployment in resource-
limited systems [8], [9].

B. Data Collection and Preprocessing
The dataset contained 1,024 RGB/PNG images at256×256
pixels, with 700 Normal Skin and 324 Lumpy Skin images.
Six images were excluded because of conflicting class labels
identified during the dataset audit, leaving 1,018 images for
analysis. To avoid data leakage, exact duplicates and additional
near-duplicates were identified through perceptual hashing.
The final dataset was split into training (711 images),
validation (154 images) and test (153 images) sets by stratified
class allocation with a fixed random seed (42). Samples that
were near-duplicates were kept within the same split. All
images were resized to224×224pixels and standardized
with ImageNet statistics. Training used data augmentation with
random horizontal flipping, random cropping and resizing,
rotations up to 10 degrees and colour jitter. These procedures
can increase variation during learning but cannot ensure gen-
eralization to independent farms and animals [11], [14].
Fig. 2. Vertical multiway data preprocessing and evaluation workflow.
C. Model Development and Selection
Two ImageNet-pretrained architectures were considered:
EfficientNet-B0 [8] and MobileNetV3-Large [9]. Each archi-
tecture was trained with both cross-entropy loss and inverse-
frequency class-weighted cross-entropy loss, giving four con-
figurations. Training used the AdamW optimizer with a learn-
ing rate of3×10 −4, weight decay of10 −4, batch size 16 and a
cosine learning-rate schedule. Training ran for up to 8 epochs,
with early stopping after 3 epochs without improvement in
validation macro-F1.
The model with the highest validation macro-F1 was se-
lected for further testing. On both validation and test sets,
classification accuracy, balanced accuracy, macro-F1, class-
specific precision and recall, ROC-AUC and PR-AUC were
calculated. Following ongoing explainability research [12],
Grad-CAM was used to provide visual explanations for pre-
dictions. The selected PyTorch model was exported to ONNX
format for CPU-based inference.

IV. EXPERIMENTALSETUP
A. Computational Environment
GPU dependence was eliminated by using CPU-only exe-
cution with one CPU thread. The same random seed, prepro-
cessing pipeline, data splits and optimization parameters were
used for all configurations. Parameters, checkpoint counts,
checkpoint sizes and single-image inference latency were
recorded. Two PyTorch predictions were compared with each
other and with ONNX Runtime predictions to assess export
consistency.
B. Comparative Evaluation
Each configuration was evaluated on the same held-out
test set. EfficientNet-B0 with class weighting achieved the
highest validation macro-F1 (95.46%) and validation accuracy
(96.10%) and was therefore selected. On the test set, this
configuration achieved 90.85% accuracy, 89.25% macro-F1,
88.81% balanced accuracy, 91.98% ROC-AUC and 90.94%
PR-AUC. Recall for Lumpy Skin was 83.33%, meaning that
a few disease-positive images were missed.
The baseline MobileNetV3-Large model without class
weighting achieved 91.50% test accuracy and a median Py-
Torch inference time of 14.22 ms, versus 26.27 ms for the
selected EfficientNet-B0. These results illustrate a trade-off
between validation performance and computational cost. The
accuracy differences, however, are small and do not indicate
any significant superiority in accuracy.
C. Deployment and Reliability Assessment
ONNX Runtime inference was evaluated on 307 validation
and test images. It achieved a median latency of 19.92 ms
and a 95th-percentile latency of 27.67 ms, compared with
PyTorch’s median latency of 55.78 ms and 95th-percentile
latency of 63.74 ms in this benchmark. Predicted classes were
identical, with a maximum absolute probability difference
of approximately2.04×10 −6. The findings show numeric
coherence and faster computation in the test computer, but
they do not prove better diagnostic precision.
The findings must be interpreted carefully, adhering to
clinical-AI reporting recommendations [15]-[17]. The test set
consisted of only 153 images, and animal/farm independence
was not tested, neither did the authors conduct any external
validation. The system is supposed to be used for initial
screening studies using imaging data only.

C. Clinical Interpretation and Limitations
The findings suggest that VetVision AI may support initial
image-based screening, but it should not be regarded as a
confirmed diagnosis by a veterinarian. Although Grad-CAM
can highlight image regions that influence the predictions, no
visual explanation can guarantee pathological validity [12],
[20]. Even when discrimination is high, reliable predictions
depend on calibration and uncertainty assessment [19]. The
key strengths demonstrated are efficient ONNX inference,
consistent exported predictions and good binary classification
performance. Independent animal-level and farm-level testing,
validation of false-negative rates and prospective veterinary
validation are still needed before clinical deployment [15]–
[17].

A. Conclusion
This research work presents VetVision AI, which is a
deep learning-based screening tool that can classify cattle
skin images into two categories - Normal Skin and Lumpy
Skin. Two pre-trained CNNs have been compared and ONNX
Runtime has been used for CPU optimization.Experimental

B. Future Scope
Other studies may be carried out which will include more
breeds of cattle, various stages of disease, geographic location
and climate. Incorporating individual-level data with farm-
level data will aid in farm-level evaluations of model gen-
eralization. The model quantization, pruning and knowledge
distillation can be performed in order to gain efficiency and
reduce inference latency on mobile and edge devices. The
addition of calibration of confidence values and explanation
techniques will aid in enhancing the clarity of the results
and may assist in the identification of unreliable results that
should be considered by a veterinarian. With the availability of
clinically labeled data, multi-class classification can evaluate
the severity of the disease and also distinguish between LSD
and other skin diseases.
